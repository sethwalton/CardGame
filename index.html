1. Core idea

Plead the Filth is a party game for 3 to 6 adults about talking your way out of the worst arrest report ever written. Each round every player is dealt a four-card charge sheet: a Crime, a Place, a piece of Evidence and a Twist. Another player reads it out like a prosecutor. You then get 60 seconds to explain yourself to the court. Every card is true, so you cannot deny anything. You can only explain it. Once everyone has pleaded, the table votes for the funniest explanation. That player is acquitted and keeps their Crime card as a trophy.

Win condition: the player with the most acquittals (Crime trophies) after the last round wins. A tie for first is settled by a Supreme Court showdown.

3 to 6 players · ages 18+ · 4 minutes (one speed round) to 60 minutes (long game) · 87 cards and a phone timer. "Plead the Filth" is a working title. Check it for trademark conflicts before printing.

2. Full rules

What is in the box

  • 80 scenario cards in four decks of 20: Crime, Place, Evidence, Twist.
  • 1 Gavel card. Whoever holds it is the Judge.
  • 6 Plea Bargain cards, one per player.
  • A timer. Any phone works.

Setup

  1. Shuffle each deck on its own. Put the four decks face down in a row in reading order: Crime, Place, Evidence, Twist. Leave room for a discard pile next to each.
  2. Give every player one Plea Bargain card, face up.
  3. The player who was most recently pulled over, fined or arrested takes the Gavel and is the first Judge. If nobody will admit to anything, the youngest player takes it.
  4. Agree how many rounds to play, using the table below.

Speed trial: one round, 30-second pleas, no cross-examination. About 4 minutes with three players.

The three roles

Defendant
The player whose turn it is. Everyone is the Defendant once per round.
Prosecutor
The player on the Defendant's left. Deals and reads the charges, runs the timer, and asks one question afterwards.
Judge
Whoever holds the Gavel. The Judge still pleads like everyone else, but goes last, calls the vote, and breaks ties.

A round

A round is one hearing per player, then one vote. The player on the Judge's left is the first Defendant. Play passes to the left, so the Judge pleads last.

Each hearing has four steps.

  1. The charges. The Prosecutor takes the top card of each deck and turns them over one at a time, reading them out as one sentence: "You were arrested for [Crime] [Place]. You were found in possession of [Evidence]. And to make matters worse: [Twist]." The four cards stay face up in front of the Defendant. This is their charge sheet.
  2. Plea Bargain (optional). Before the timer starts, the Defendant may turn their Plea Bargain card face down to throw out one card from the charge sheet and draw a replacement from the same deck. The replacement stays, even if it is worse. Each player can do this once per game.
  3. The plea. The Prosecutor starts a 60-second timer. The Defendant explains to the court why they are innocent, justified or badly misunderstood. Everything on the charge sheet is true and it all happened. You may not deny a card. You may only explain it. You can stop early by saying "I rest my case." When the timer runs out, finish your sentence and stop.
  4. Cross-examination. The Prosecutor asks one follow-up question. The Defendant answers in a sentence or two. There are no follow-ups to the follow-up.

Order in the court. Nobody interrupts a plea. Laughing, groaning and booing are encouraged.

The verdict

  1. When every player has pleaded, the Judge counts "three, two, one, verdict." On "verdict" every player points at the Defendant whose plea was the funniest. You cannot point at yourself.
  2. The Defendant with the most votes is found Not Guilty. They keep their Crime card face up in front of them as a trophy worth 1 point, and discard their other three cards.
  3. Everyone else is guilty. Their four cards go to the discard piles.
  4. The acquitted player takes the Gavel and is the Judge for the next round. If they already hold it, they keep it.

Ties in a vote

The Judge's vote breaks the tie. Of the tied players, whoever the Judge is pointing at wins. If the Judge is pointing at someone who is not in the tie, the Judge points again at one of the tied players. The Judge can never rule for themself, so a Judge who is part of a tie always loses it.

What each card does

Crime (20 cards)
What you did. Read first, after "You were arrested for". The only card worth points: the acquitted player keeps theirs as a 1-point trophy.
Place (20 cards)
Where it happened. Read straight after the Crime so the two form one phrase.
Evidence (20 cards)
What the police found on you. Read after "You were found in possession of".
Twist (20 cards)
The detail that makes it worse. Read last, after "And to make matters worse".
The Gavel (1 card)
Marks the Judge. The Judge pleads last, calls the vote, and breaks ties, but never in their own favour. It passes to each round's winner, which makes it a small handicap on whoever is ahead.
Plea Bargain (6 cards)
Once per game, before your timer starts, swap one card of your charge sheet for a new one from the same deck. Turn the Plea Bargain face down when used.

Strike it from the record. This is a filthy game and some cards will land badly with some tables. Any player can ask for any card to be struck at any time, with no explanation needed. Discard it and draw a replacement. This does not use a Plea Bargain.

Empty deck. If a deck runs out, shuffle its discard pile to make a new one. Trophies stay with their owners.

End of the game and scoring

The game ends after the agreed number of rounds. Each Crime trophy is 1 point. Most points wins.

Tiebreaker: the Supreme Court

  1. The players tied for first are the finalists. Each gets a fresh charge sheet and a 30-second plea, starting with the finalist nearest the Judge's left. There are no Plea Bargains and no cross-examination.
  2. With two finalists, only the other players vote. With three or more finalists, everyone votes, for a finalist other than themself.
  3. A tied vote is broken by the Judge in the usual way. The winner of the showdown wins the game.

4. Balance check

Why no card or strategy dominates

  • Cards are prompts, not powers. Points only come from votes. No card scores, blocks or steals, so there is nothing to draft or hoard. Four independent decks give 160,000 possible charge sheets, so nobody can prepare material.
  • Hard draws have an escape. The Plea Bargain lets each player fix one bad card per game. It is deliberately a single use, so choosing when to spend it is a real decision.
  • The leader carries the Gavel. The last winner is the Judge, and the Judge cannot win a tie. With three players voting at random, the Judge wins about 25% of rounds and the other two about 37.5% each. In return the Judge pleads last, which is the best seat in a voting game. The net effect is a gentle catch-up rule.
  • Voting is simultaneous. Pointing on a count stops players from piling onto whoever is already winning the vote.

Likely problem spots

  • One funny friend wins everything. This is true of every game in the genre. The Gavel handicap softens it. If it is still lopsided, see the playtest knobs.
  • Vote trading at three players. Two players can agree never to vote for the leader. Simultaneous pointing makes this awkward but not impossible. A secret ballot does not fix it either. Treat it as a table-manners issue.
  • The Gavel may feel like a punishment. A Judge who ties loses every time. If playtesters resent it, pass the Gavel to the left each round instead of to the winner.
  • Cards that bring their own prop. Some Crimes already contain an object (the dildo, the bouncy castle, the megaphone). With an Evidence card on top, the Defendant has two props to explain. That is usually funnier, but watch whether those Crimes get bargained away more often.
  • Twists about the arrest instead of the act. "The arresting officer is your ex" and "You shat in the back of the squad car" happen after the crime, so some players will tack them on at the end. Watch whether they get ignored.
  • Overlaps. Two cards can say nearly the same thing, such as greasing yourself up naked while wearing only a sock. This reads as a joke in most cases. If it falls flat, the Plea Bargain covers it.
  • The darkest cards. Cannibalism, the corpse and the burial kit will split tables. The strike rule exists for this. Track which cards get struck.
  • Dead air. Sixty seconds is long for a shy player. Resting early is allowed and should be normal.
  • Downtime at six players. A round is twelve minutes and you plead once. The Prosecutor role keeps one more person busy each turn. If six still drags, cut the plea to 45 seconds.

5. Example round

Three players sit clockwise: Maya, Dev, Rosa. Maya holds the Gavel, so she is the Judge. Dev is on her left and goes first.

  1. Dev's hearing. Rosa is on Dev's left, so she is his Prosecutor. She flips four cards and reads: "You were arrested for offering handjobs in exchange for chicken nuggets, at a nursing home bingo night. You were found in possession of a cooler labeled NOT ORGANS. And to make matters worse: you were stone-cold sober. Every test came back clean." Dev keeps all four. He explains that he is a licensed massage therapist who accepts payment in protein, that the cooler holds the nuggets, that the label was there to stop the residents asking, and that sobriety is a job requirement. Rosa asks her one question: "Why were the nuggets still warm?" Dev: "Body heat. I refuse to elaborate."
  2. Rosa's hearing. Maya is her Prosecutor. The charges: joyriding a stolen hearse with the casket still in the back, at the altar midway through her own wedding vows, in possession of six positive pregnancy tests (none of them hers), and her mom was there the whole time, cheering her on. Rosa hates the pregnancy tests. She turns her Plea Bargain face down, discards that card, and draws a new Evidence card: somebody else's prosthetic leg. She has to keep it. She pleads that the hearse was the "something borrowed."
  3. Maya's hearing. As Judge she goes last. Dev is her Prosecutor. She gets a meth lab in a bouncy castle, live on the local morning news, a heavily sedated swan, and a sexy Abraham Lincoln costume. She pleads that it was a science segment for the weather slot.
  4. The verdict. Maya counts down. Dev points at Rosa. Rosa points at Maya. Maya points at Dev. That is one vote each: a three-way tie. The Judge's vote breaks it. Maya is pointing at Dev, so Dev is Not Guilty. He keeps his Solicitation card as a trophy, everything else is discarded, and Dev takes the Gavel. In round 2 Dev is the Judge and pleads last. Rosa, on his left, goes first.

6. Edge cases

Can I say I did not do it?
No. Every card is a fact. You can claim a good reason, a misunderstanding or a noble motive, but the events happened.
Two of my cards contradict each other.
Both are true. For example, you greased yourself up naked and you were also dressed as a sexy Abraham Lincoln. Explaining how is your problem.
When exactly can I use my Plea Bargain?
After all four cards are read and before your timer starts. Not during or after your plea.
The replacement card is worse.
You keep it. There is one swap per player per game.
Can I bargain away the Crime?
Yes. Any one of the four cards.
Can I use a Plea Bargain in the Supreme Court?
No.
The Prosecutor asked a brutal question to sink me.
That is allowed. The Prosecutor is also your rival. In practice a hard question gives you the best laugh of the turn. It must be one question, not a speech.
Someone pointed at themself, or did not point.
That vote does not count. Have them point again.
Three players, one vote each.
It is a three-way tie. The Judge's vote decides it, so whoever the Judge pointed at wins.
The Judge is one of the tied players.
The Judge cannot win a tie. If one other player is tied with them, that player wins. If several are, the Judge's vote picks between them.
The Judge voted for someone who is not in the tie.
The Judge points again, at one of the tied players other than themself. This can only happen with four or more players.
The round's winner already holds the Gavel.
They keep it.
What happens to the winner's other cards?
The winner keeps only the Crime. The Place, Evidence and Twist are discarded.
A deck ran out.
Shuffle that deck's discards into a new deck. Trophies are not shuffled back in.
The timer ran out mid-sentence.
Finish the sentence. Do not start another.
In the Supreme Court with two finalists, the jury vote is tied.
The Judge breaks it as usual. If the Judge is one of the finalists, the other finalist wins.
A card makes someone at the table uncomfortable.
Strike it. Discard and redraw, no questions asked, and it does not cost a Plea Bargain.
Someone has to leave mid-game.
Set their trophies aside and keep playing if three or more remain. If they held the Gavel, it goes to the player on their left.
Can two people play?
No. There is nobody left to be the jury.

7. Card layout

Standard poker size, 2.5 × 3.5 inches, portrait. Add 0.125 inch of bleed on every side for the printer (2.75 × 3.75 inch artwork) and keep all text 0.125 inch inside the cut line. The cards in this prototype follow the same layout.

  1. Reading-order number. Top left, in a circle. 1 Crime, 2 Place, 3 Evidence, 4 Twist. It tells the Prosecutor which card to read next and tells players which deck goes where on the table.
  2. Deck icon and deck name. In the colour band across the top, 0.4 inch tall. Colour, icon and name all identify the deck, so it works for colour-blind players.
  3. Card ID. Top right, small monospace. One letter and two digits (C07). Used for playtest notes and reprints.
  4. Lead-in line. Small caps under the band. It is the sentence starter the Prosecutor reads before the card text.
  5. Effect text. The largest text on the card, bold, left aligned, about 14 pt. Keep it under 90 characters so it can be read aloud at a glance.
  6. Art window. Lower quarter, about 0.85 inch tall. A spot illustration of the card, or the deck icon on a height-chart background if a card has no art yet.
  7. Name and point value. Bottom strip. Card name on the left. Crime cards also show "Worth 1 pt" on the right, because an acquitted player keeps theirs as a trophy.

Backs. Each deck has its own back in the deck colour, with the icon, the deck name and the reading-order number, so the four decks can be sorted and laid out in order without turning them over. The Gavel and Plea Bargain cards use the same front layout with rules text in place of the effect text and no art window.

8. Playtest plan

Play the first three games in this prototype with three people. The app records how long each plea and round takes, which cards are drawn, which are on winning sheets, and which get bargained away. Use the flag button on any card during play to mark it for a rewrite.

Game 1: does the turn work?

  • Can the Prosecutor read the four cards as one sentence without stumbling?
  • How long do pleas actually run? If most players rest before 40 seconds, the timer is too long.
  • Does the cross-examination question get a laugh, or does it stall?
  • Does anyone forget who the Prosecutor is? If so the role needs a marker.

Game 2: are the cards good?

  • Which cards get a laugh just from being read? Approve those.
  • Which cards get ignored in the plea? Those are weak. Flag them.
  • Which cards get bargained away or struck? Rewrite or cut them.
  • Is any one deck weaker than the others?

Game 3: is it fair?

  • How many rounds end in a tie? More than one in three is too many.
  • Does the Judge feel punished? Does the same player hold the Gavel all game?
  • Is the final score close, and did the last round matter?
  • Did anyone hold their Plea Bargain all game? If most did, it is too precious or not needed.
