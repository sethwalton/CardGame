1. Core idea

Plead the Filth is a party game for 3 to 6 adults about building the worst arrest report ever written. A case is four cards read as one sentence: a Crime, a Place, a piece of Evidence and a Twist. On your turn you secretly deal yourself three cases, keep the one you think is the most ridiculous, and may swap one of its cards for something worse. When everyone has built a case, the court goes to trial. Every case is revealed and read out, and the players, acting as prosecutors, vote for the most outrageous one. The owner of each top-voted case gets one conviction.

Win condition: the player with the most convictions (Crime cards on their rap sheet) after the last round wins. Players tied for first share the win.

3 to 6 players · ages 18+ · about 5 minutes (one speed round) to 60 minutes (long game).

2. Full rules

What is in the box

Words used in these rules

Case
One card from each deck, read in order as a sentence: "You were arrested for [Crime] [Place]. You were found in possession of [Evidence]. And to make matters worse: [Twist]."
Prosecutors
Everyone, during the trial. Each player reads out the case of the player on their right, and every player votes.

A round

A round has two phases. First every player builds a case, one turn each. Then all the cases go on trial together.

Phase one: building cases

Start with the first player for this round, then go clockwise. Keep your cards hidden from the other players the whole time.

  1. Deal three cases. Deal yourself three rows of four cards face down in front of you, one card from each deck per row. Each row is a case. Look at your rows without showing anyone.
  2. Keep one case. Pick up the row you think is the most ridiculous. You must keep it exactly as dealt: you cannot move cards between rows. The other two rows go face down onto their discard piles.
  3. Swap one card (optional). You may discard one card from your case, look at the top three cards of the same deck, and keep the one you like best. The other two go onto the discard pile. You cannot get the discarded card back.
  4. Lock it in. Hold your case face down. Your turn is over. Do not show it to anyone until the trial.

Phase two: the trial

  1. When every player has built a case, the trial follows the same order.
  2. Each player in turn lays their case face up. The player on their left reads it out as one sentence, like a prosecutor reading the charges. The owner offers no defence. Laughing, groaning and booing are encouraged.
  3. When every case has been read, every player casts one vote for the funniest case. You cannot vote for your own. In person, point at the same time on a count of three.
  4. The case with the most votes is found Guilty. Record one conviction and its Crime card name on the owner’s rap sheet.
  5. Discard all the cases after scoring.

Ties in a vote

When multiple cases tie for the most votes, each of their owners earns one conviction.

What each card does

Crime
What you did. Read first, after "You were arrested for". The only card worth points: each convicted player records 1 point on their rap sheet.
Place
Where it happened. Read straight after the Crime so the two form one phrase.
Evidence
What the police found on you. Read after "You were found in possession of".
Twist
The detail that makes it worse. Read last, after "And to make matters worse".

Strike it from the record. This is a filthy game and some cards will land badly with some tables. Any player can ask for any card to be struck at any time, with no explanation needed. Discard it and replace it with the top card of the same deck. This does not count as your swap.

Empty deck. If a deck runs out, shuffle its discard pile to make a new one. Keep each player’s rap sheet as a written score.

End of the game and scoring

The game ends after the agreed number of rounds. Each Crime card on your rap sheet is 1 point. The player with the most points wins. Players tied for first share the win.

4. Balance check

Why no card or strategy dominates

  • Cards are prompts, not powers. Points only come from votes. No card scores, blocks or steals, so there is nothing to draft or hoard. Four independent decks give possible cases.
  • Everyone gets the same choices. Each turn you see three cases and, with the swap, three more options for one card. A bad draw costs less than in a one-case game, because you choose the best of three and can fix its weakest card.
  • Cases stay hidden until the trial. Nobody can see another case and react to it, so going earlier or later in a round gives no information advantage.
  • Voting is simultaneous. Pointing on a count stops players from piling onto whoever is already winning the vote.

Likely problem spots

  • The cards carry all the humour. With no spoken defence, a round is only as funny as the cases on the table. Weak cards matter much more than before, and so does how the four decks chain into a sentence. This is the first thing to watch in playtesting.
  • Waiting for your turn. Turns are private and one at a time, so with six players you wait through five turns before the trial. If this drags, try the simultaneous building variant in the playtest plan.
  • Repeat draws. A long game or a smaller print selection can reach the discard pile. Fewer cases per turn or a blind swap will stretch each shuffle further.
  • Slow turns. Comparing three cases and then three swap options can take a while for some players. Watch the average turn time on the end screen.
  • One player wins everything. A player with a better eye for the cards can run away with it. If playtests are consistently lopsided, see the playtest knobs.
  • Vote trading at three players. Two players can agree never to vote for the leader. Simultaneous pointing makes this awkward but not impossible. Treat it as a table-manners issue.
  • Cards that bring their own prop. Some Crimes already contain an object (the dildo, the bouncy castle, the megaphone). With an Evidence card on top that is usually funnier, but watch whether those cases get kept or passed over.
  • The darkest cards. Cannibalism, the corpse and the burial kit will split tables. The strike rule exists for this. Track which cards get struck.

5. Example round

Three players sit clockwise: Maya, Dev, Rosa. Dev is the starting player for this round.

  1. Dev's turn. Dev deals himself three rows of four cards and looks at them. Case 2 is the strongest: offering handjobs in exchange for chicken nuggets, at a nursing home bingo night, a cooler labeled "NOT ORGANS", and he was stone-cold sober. He keeps it and discards the other two rows. The Twist feels flat, so he swaps it: he discards it, looks at the top three Twist cards, keeps "Your mom was there the whole time, cheering you on," and discards the other two. He hides his case.
  2. Rosa's turn. Rosa deals her three cases. Case 1 is joyriding a stolen hearse with the casket still in the back, at the altar midway through her own wedding vows, holding somebody else's prosthetic leg, and she live-streamed it to 40,000 viewers. She likes it as it is, keeps it, and does not swap.
  3. Maya's turn. She keeps a case with a meth lab in a bouncy castle, live on the local morning news, and a heavily sedated swan. She swaps the Twist for a sexy Abraham Lincoln costume.
  4. The trial. Dev lays his case down first and Rosa, on his left, reads it out. Then Rosa's case, read by Maya. Then Maya's, read by Dev. Nobody explains anything.
  5. The verdict. Dev votes for Rosa. Rosa votes for Maya. Maya votes for Rosa. Rosa wins with two votes and records one conviction for Grand Theft Hearse. All cases are discarded. Rosa takes the first turn in round 2.

6. Edge cases

Can I move cards between my three cases?
No. You keep one case exactly as dealt. Your swap is the only way to change it.
Do I have to swap?
No. The swap is optional.
Can I swap more than one card?
No. One card per turn.
The three swap options are all worse than my old card.
You still keep one of the three. The card you discarded is gone.
Can I swap the Crime?
Yes. Any one of the four cards.
A deck runs out while I am dealing or swapping.
Shuffle that deck's discard pile into a new deck and keep going. Rap sheets are never shuffled back in.
Can I talk about my case before the trial?
You can talk all you like, but you cannot show it to anyone.
Can I explain my case at the trial?
No. The case speaks for itself. Reactions are fine.
Someone pointed at their own case, or did not point.
That vote does not count. Have them point again.
Three players, one vote each.
Each player earns one conviction.
What happens to the winner's other cards?
The winner keeps only the Crime. The Place, Evidence and Twist are discarded.
A card makes someone at the table uncomfortable.
Strike it. Discard it and replace it from the same deck, no questions asked. It does not use up your swap.
Someone has to leave mid-game.
Set their rap sheet aside and keep playing if three or more remain.
Can two people play?
No. With only two cases there is nobody to break the deadlock: each player would always vote for the other.

7. Card layout

Standard poker size, 2.5 × 3.5 inches, portrait. Add 0.125 inch of bleed on every side for the printer (2.75 × 3.75 inch artwork) and keep all text 0.125 inch inside the cut line. The cards in this prototype follow the same layout.

  1. Reading-order number. Top left, in a circle. 1 Crime, 2 Place, 3 Evidence, 4 Twist. It tells the Prosecutor which card to read next and tells players which deck goes where on the table.
  2. Deck icon and deck name. In the colour band across the top, 0.4 inch tall. Colour, icon and name all identify the deck, so it works for colour-blind players.
  3. Card ID. Top right, small monospace. One letter and two digits (C07). Used for playtest notes and reprints.
  4. Lead-in line. Small caps under the band. It is the sentence starter the Prosecutor reads before the card text.
  5. Effect text. The largest text on the card, bold, left aligned, about 14 pt. Keep it under 90 characters so it can be read aloud at a glance.
  6. Art window. Lower quarter, about 0.85 inch tall. A spot illustration of the card, or the deck icon on a height-chart background if a card has no art yet.
  7. Name and point value. Bottom strip. Card name on the left. Crime cards also show "Worth 1 pt" on the right, because the round's winner keeps theirs on their rap sheet.

Backs. Each deck has its own back in the deck colour, with the icon, the deck name and the reading-order number, so the four decks can be sorted and laid out in order without turning them over.

8. Playtest plan

Play the first three games in this prototype with three people. The app records how long each turn and round takes, which cards are drawn, which are in the case a player keeps, which are on winning cases, and which get swapped out. Use the flag button on any card during the trial to mark it for a rewrite.

Game 1: does the turn work?

  • How long does a turn take? The end screen shows the average. Over two minutes is too slow.
  • Is passing the device for private turns awkward, or does the waiting feel fine?
  • Do players use the swap? How often is shown on the end screen.
  • Does the reader stumble when reading a case as one sentence?

Game 2: are the cards good?

  • Which cards get a laugh at the trial? Approve those.
  • Which cards are drawn often but never kept? Those are weak. The Kept column in the Cards tab's table view shows this.
  • Which cards get swapped out or struck? Rewrite or cut them.
  • Is any one deck weaker than the others?

Game 3: is it fair?

  • How many rounds end in a tie? More than one in three is too many.
  • Is the final score close, and did the last round matter?
  • Did the same card show up twice in one trial?
